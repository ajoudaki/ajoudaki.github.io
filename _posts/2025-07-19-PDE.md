Complex systems composed of many interacting, random parts are ubiquitous in science and engineering. From traffic flow on a highway to pedestrian crowds in a city, tracking each individual agent is often a hopeless task. The goal of theoretical modeling is to find a simpler, macroscopic law that governs the system's average behavior. This "mean-field" approach shifts our focus from the chaotic micro-scale to the predictable macro-scale.

This post tells the story of one such journey. We begin with a seemingly simple road where intersections have random capacities for letting cars through. This simple setup quickly reveals complex, unpredictable traffic jams. We will show how, by adopting a continuous perspective and introducing a single, powerful physical assumption, this entire stochastic process can be described by a solvable Partial Differential Equation (PDE).

Our derivation will take us from a discrete, random process to a continuous, deterministic one, culminating in a closed-form solution that predicts how traffic queues form and grow over time. This journey is a microcosm of how theoretical physics and applied mathematics can be used to find order in chaos.

---

### The Setup: A Stochastic Traffic System

Let's begin by defining the system precisely.

**The Stochastic Process.** We consider a long road with a series of intersections, indexed $k=0, 1, 2, \ldots$. During each time step $t$ (e.g., a green light cycle), the system operates as follows:

- **Inflow:** The first intersection ($k=0$) receives a deterministic inflow of **1 unit** of traffic (representing a steady stream of cars).
- **Capacity:** Each intersection $k$ has a random capacity for the cycle, $X_k(t)$, drawn independently from a uniform distribution, $X_k(t) \sim \text{Unif}[0,1]$. This represents the variable number of cars it can let through.
- **Outflow:** Intersection $k$ allows a number of cars to pass equal to the **minimum** of its capacity and the total cars available to it (its own queue plus new arrivals). This becomes its outflow, $F_k(t)$.
- **Queue:** Cars that are available but cannot pass remain in the queue for the next cycle, $Q_k(t)$.

**Random Variables.** The system is governed by the evolution of two key random variables: the **queue length** $Q_k(t)$ and the **outflow** $F_k(t)$. Their dynamics are given by:

\begin{align}
F_k(t) &= \min(X_k(t), Q_k(t-1) + F_{k-1}(t)) \cr
Q_k(t) &= Q_k(t-1) + F_{k-1}(t) - F_k(t)
\end{align}

where $F_{-1}(t) \equiv 1$ represents the inflow to the first intersection.

Our goal is to find analytical expressions for the **expected values** of these quantities over many independent runs:
- Expected Queue Length: $q_k(t) = E[Q_k(t)]$
- Expected Outflow: $f_k(t) = E[F_k(t)]$

---

### The Mean-Field Approach

The first step in taming this complexity is to move from a discrete model to a continuous one, treating the intersection index $k$ (as distance) and time $t$ as continuous variables.

**The PDE Approximation.** The discrete conservation law for cars, $q_k(t) - q_k(t-1) = f_{k-1}(t) - f_k(t)$, can be approximated by a continuous Partial Differential Equation (PDE). The change in time becomes a time derivative, $\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}$, while the change across intersections becomes a spatial derivative (the net flow), $-\frac{\partial f}{\partial k}$. This gives the fundamental conservation law:

$$\frac{\partial q(k,t)}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial f(k,t)}{\partial k}$$

**The Key Physical Assumption.** To solve this, we need a "closure relation"—a fundamental diagram—that connects the expected outflow $f(k,t)$ to the expected queue length $q(k,t)$. This is where we introduce our core physical assumption, motivated by queueing theory:

- **The queue length $Q(k,t)$ is a random variable that follows an exponential distribution.**

The mean of this distribution is the expected queue length $q(k,t)$. For an exponential distribution with rate $\lambda$, the mean is $1/\lambda$. Thus, we have the crucial link: $q(k,t) = 1/\lambda(k,t)$.

---

### Deriving the Flow-Density Relationship: The Traffic Physics

With our key assumption in place, we can derive the relationship between outflow and queue length. This refined model accurately reflects the simulation by including the inflow from the previous intersection, which we approximate as a random variable $Y \sim \text{Unif}[0,1]$.

The expected outflow is a function of the intersection's own stochastic queue $Q_k$ and the stochastic inflow $Y$:

$$f(k,t) = E[\min(X_k, Q_k + Y)]$$

where $X_k \sim \text{Unif}[0,1]$, $Q_k \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda_k)$, and $Y \sim \text{Unif}[0,1]$.

**The Asymptotic Approximation.** The exact integral for this expectation is intractable. We therefore find its behavior for large queues ($q \to \infty$, which means $\lambda \to 0$), which corresponds to heavy traffic. This involves first finding the expectation for a fixed queue length $z$, which gives the function $g(z) = \frac{(z-1)^3}{6} + \frac{1}{2}$, and then averaging this over the exponential distribution of the queue. The asymptotic result is:

$$f(q) \approx \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{24q}$$

This provides the accurate, tractable relationship we need. For a detailed derivation of this result, please see the appendix of this post.

**The PDE.** We now substitute this physically accurate flow-density law into our conservation PDE:

\begin{align}
\frac{\partial q}{\partial t} &= -\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{24q}\right) \cr
&= -\frac{1}{24q^2}\frac{\partial q}{\partial k}
\end{align}

To summarize we hace the PDE:

\begin{align}
\boxed{
  \frac{\partial q}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{24q^2}\frac{\partial q}{\partial k} 
}
\end{align}

This is the final, solvable PDE that governs the system's long-term traffic dynamics.

---

### The Final PDE and its Solution

We now present the complete, first-principles solution to the PDE $\frac{\partial q}{\partial t} = -\frac{1}{24q^2}\frac{\partial q}{\partial k}$ with the initial condition that all queues are empty, $q_k(0)=0$.

**Step 1: The Ansatz.** We assume a solution of the form $q(k,t) = c(k)\sqrt{t}$, which separates the variables. Substituting this into the PDE yields:

\begin{align}
\frac{c(k)}{2\sqrt{t}} &= -\frac{1}{24(c(k)\sqrt{t})^2} \left(\frac{d c(k)}{dk}\sqrt{t}\right) \cr
&= -\frac{c'(k)}{24c(k)^2\sqrt{t}}
\end{align}

**Step 2: Solving for the Coefficient Function $c(k)$.** The time dependence cancels, leaving a separable ODE for $c(k)$:

\begin{align}
12c(k)^3 &= -c'(k) \cr
-12\,dk &= \frac{dc}{c^3}
\end{align}

Integrating both sides:

\begin{align}
\int -12\,dk &= \int c^{-3}\,dc \cr
-12k + A &= \frac{c^{-2}}{-2} \cr
c(k)^2 &= \frac{1}{24k - 2A} \cr
c(k) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{24k + B}}
\end{align}

where $B=-2A$ is the constant of integration.

**Step 3: Applying the Boundary Condition.** To find the constant $B$, we must match our continuous solution to the known behavior of the first intersection ($k=1$). Its dynamics are described by the discrete ODE using our derived physics:

$$\frac{dq_1}{dt} = f_0 - f_1 \approx \frac{1}{2} - \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{24q_1}\right) = \frac{1}{24q_1}$$

Solving this ODE for $q_1(t)$ with the initial condition $q_1(0)=0$:

\begin{align}
24q_1\,dq_1 &= dt \cr
\int_0^{q_1(t)} 24q_1'\,dq_1' &= \int_0^t d\tau \cr
12q_1(t)^2 &= t
\end{align}

This proves that $q_1(t) = \sqrt{t/12}$. Therefore, our coefficient function must satisfy $c(1) = 1/\sqrt{12}$:

\begin{align}
c(1) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{12}} \cr
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{12}}\right)^2 &= \frac{1}{24(1)+B} \cr
\frac{1}{12} &= \frac{1}{24+B} \cr
B &= -12
\end{align}

**Step 4: Final Solution for Queue Length.** With the constant $B$ determined, we have the complete solution for the expected queue length:

$$\boxed{q(k,t) = \sqrt{\frac{t}{24k-12}}}$$

By substituting our solution for $q(k,t)$ back into the flow-density law, we also find the solution for the expected outflow:

\begin{align}
\boxed{f(k,t) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{\sqrt{24k-12}}{24\sqrt{t}}}
\end{align}

This journey, from a complex stochastic model of individual cars and intersections to a simple, elegant closed-form solution, showcases the power of theoretical modeling. By making a physically motivated mean-field assumption—that the complex distribution of queue lengths could be approximated as exponential—we were able to derive a continuous model that accurately captures the long-term behavior of the entire traffic system. 

The final equations reveal a clear picture: queue lengths at each intersection grow with the square root of time, while the outflow slowly approaches the steady-state value of $1/2$, with both dynamics governed by the intersection's position along the road. This elegant mathematical structure emerges from what initially appeared to be intractable stochastic chaos, demonstrating the profound insights that can be gained into traffic dynamics through careful theoretical analysis.

// Wait for Plotly to load
function waitForPlotly(callback) {
    if (typeof Plotly !== 'undefined') {
        callback();
    } else {
        setTimeout(() => waitForPlotly(callback), 100);
    }
}

waitForPlotly(() => {
    const colors = ['#ef4444', '#3b82f6', '#10b981', '#f59e0b', '#8b5cf6'];

    window.simulateTraffic = function(numIntersections, numTimesteps, numRuns) {
        const qSum = Array(numIntersections).fill(null).map(() => Array(numTimesteps).fill(0));
        const fSum = Array(numIntersections).fill(null).map(() => Array(numTimesteps).fill(0));
        
        for (let run = 0; run < numRuns; run++) {
            const q = Array(numIntersections).fill(null).map(() => Array(numTimesteps + 1).fill(0));
            const f = Array(numIntersections).fill(null).map(() => Array(numTimesteps).fill(0));
            
            for (let t = 0; t < numTimesteps; t++) {
                const cap = Array(numIntersections).fill(0).map(() => Math.random());
                for (let k = 0; k < numIntersections; k++) {
                    const avail = q[k][t] + (k === 0 ? 1.0 : f[k-1][t]);
                    f[k][t] = Math.min(cap[k], avail);
                    q[k][t + 1] = avail - f[k][t];
                }
            }
            
            for (let k = 0; k < numIntersections; k++) {
                for (let t = 0; t < numTimesteps; t++) {
                    qSum[k][t] += q[k][t + 1];
                    fSum[k][t] += f[k][t];
                }
            }
        }
        
        return {
            queue: qSum.map(row => row.map(val => val / numRuns)),
            flow: fSum.map(row => row.map(val => val / numRuns))
        };
    }

    function theoreticalPred(m, times) {
        const qT = Array(m).fill(null).map(() => Array(times.length).fill(0));
        const fT = Array(m).fill(null).map(() => Array(times.length).fill(0));
        
        for (let t_idx = 0; t_idx < times.length; t_idx++) {
            const time = times[t_idx];
            for (let k = 1; k <= m; k++) {
                if (24 * k - 12 > 0) {
                    qT[k - 1][t_idx] = Math.sqrt(time / (24 * k - 12));
                    fT[k - 1][t_idx] = 0.5 - Math.sqrt(24 * k - 12) / (24 * Math.sqrt(time));
                }
            }
        }
        return { queue: qT, flow: fT };
    }

    window.updatePlots = function() {
        const m = parseInt(document.getElementById('numIntersections').value);
        const d = parseInt(document.getElementById('numTimesteps').value);
        const r = parseInt(document.getElementById('numRuns').value);
        
        const sim = simulateTraffic(m, d, r);
        const times = Array.from({length: d}, (_, i) => i + 1);
        const theory = theoreticalPred(m, times);
        
        const qTraces = [];
        const fTraces = [];
        
        for (let k = 1; k < m; k++) {
            const col = colors[k-1];
            qTraces.push(
                {x: times, y: sim.queue[k], name: `I.${k+1} (M.C.)`, line: {color: col, width: 2.5}},
                {x: times, y: theory.queue[k], name: `I.${k+1} (Th.)`, line: {color: col, dash: 'dash'}}
            );
            
            const fDistSim = sim.flow[k].map(v => Math.abs(0.5 - v));
            const fDistTheory = theory.flow[k].map(v => Math.abs(0.5 - v));
            fTraces.push(
                {x: times, y: fDistSim, name: `I.${k+1}`, line: {color: col, width: 2.5}, showlegend: false},
                {x: times, y: fDistTheory, line: {color: col, dash: 'dash'}, showlegend: false}
            );
        }
        
        const layoutQueue = {
            xaxis: {title: 'Time (cycles)'},
            yaxis: {title: 'Expected Queue Length'},
            title: 'Queue Length Evolution',
            margin: {t: 40, b: 40, l: 60, r: 20},
            legend: { y: 0.95, x: 0.05 }
        };
        
        const layoutFlow = {
            xaxis: {title: 'Time (cycles)', type: 'log'},
            yaxis: {title: 'Distance to Steady State', type: 'log'},
            title: 'Flow Rate Convergence',
            margin: {t: 40, b: 40, l: 60, r: 20}
        };
        
        Plotly.newPlot('queue-plot', qTraces, layoutQueue, {responsive: true, displayModeBar: false});
        Plotly.newPlot('flow-plot', fTraces, layoutFlow, {responsive: true, displayModeBar: false});
    }

    updatePlots();
});
</script>
