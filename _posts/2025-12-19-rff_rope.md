<h1 id="introduction">Introduction</h1>
<p>The Transformer architecture’s self-attention mechanism, being
permutation-equivariant, requires explicit positional encoding (PE)
<span class="citation" data-cites="vaswani2017attention">(<a
href="#ref-vaswani2017attention" role="doc-biblioref">Vaswani et al.
2017</a>)</span>. Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) <span
class="citation" data-cites="su2021roformer">(<a
href="#ref-su2021roformer" role="doc-biblioref">Su et al.
2021</a>)</span> has achieved widespread success by injecting relative
position information via rotation matrices <span
class="math inline">\(R(m \theta_i)\)</span>. However, RoPE’s reliance
on fixed frequencies (e.g., <span class="math inline">\(\theta_i =
10000^{-2i/d}\)</span>) <span class="citation"
data-cites="su2021roformer vaswani2017attention">(<a
href="#ref-su2021roformer" role="doc-biblioref">Su et al. 2021</a>; <a
href="#ref-vaswani2017attention" role="doc-biblioref">Vaswani et al.
2017</a>)</span> couples the model to a specific, fixed decay profile.
This rigidity restricts the flexibility needed to inject domain-specific
inductive biases or handle structured data; extensions of RoPE to
multi-dimensional settings have been explored in vision and spatial
domains <span class="citation"
data-cites="li2021learnablefourier heo2024ropevit">(<a
href="#ref-li2021learnablefourier" role="doc-biblioref">Li et al.
2021</a>; <a href="#ref-heo2024ropevit" role="doc-biblioref">Heo et al.
2024</a>)</span>.</p>
<p>In this work, we present a synthesis that unifies kernel theory with
the RoPE operator, creating a generative design methodology termed
Random Frequency RoPE (RF-RoPE). We extend the one-dimensional RoPE to
support arbitrary <span class="math inline">\(k\)</span>-dimensional
position vectors <span class="math inline">\(\mathbf{p} \in
\mathbb{R}^k\)</span>.</p>
<p>Our central contribution is the establishment of the Master Theorem
for rotational positional kernels. This theorem provides a principled
inverse design recipe: <span class="math display">\[\Phi(\Delta
\mathbf{p}) \rightarrow \text{Inverse Fourier Transform }
p(\mathbf{\omega}) \rightarrow \text{Sampled Frequencies }
\mathbf{\omega}_i.\]</span> This framework allows for the selection of
rotational frequencies that provably realize any desired shift-invariant
kernel in expectation at initialization. We further analyze how this
mechanism adapts during training to support complex attention patterns
such as shifted-local attention.</p>
<h1 id="background-and-related-work">Background and Related Work</h1>
<p>The connection between positional encoding and kernel methods is
established through Random Fourier Features (RFF) <span class="citation"
data-cites="rahimi2007random">(<a href="#ref-rahimi2007random"
role="doc-biblioref">Rahimi and Recht 2007</a>)</span> and Bochner’s
theorem <span class="citation"
data-cites="bochner1955harmonic rasmussen2006gpml">(<a
href="#ref-bochner1955harmonic" role="doc-biblioref">Bochner 1955</a>;
<a href="#ref-rasmussen2006gpml" role="doc-biblioref">Rasmussen and
Williams 2006</a>)</span>.</p>
<h2 id="foundation-the-rff-framework">Foundation: The RFF Framework</h2>
<p>Bochner’s theorem states that any continuous, shift-invariant
positive-definite kernel <span class="math inline">\(K(\mathbf{x},
\mathbf{y}) = \Phi(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y})\)</span> can be represented
as the Fourier transform of a non-negative measure <span
class="math inline">\(p(\mathbf{\omega})\)</span> <span class="citation"
data-cites="bochner1955harmonic rasmussen2006gpml">(<a
href="#ref-bochner1955harmonic" role="doc-biblioref">Bochner 1955</a>;
<a href="#ref-rasmussen2006gpml" role="doc-biblioref">Rasmussen and
Williams 2006</a>)</span>: <span class="math display">\[\Phi(\Delta
\mathbf{p}) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^k} e^{i \Delta \mathbf{p}^T
\mathbf{\omega}} p(\mathbf{\omega}) d\mathbf{\omega}.\]</span> The RFF
methodology uses Monte Carlo sampling from the spectral density <span
class="math inline">\(p(\mathbf{\omega})\)</span> to approximate the
kernel <span class="citation" data-cites="rahimi2007random">(<a
href="#ref-rahimi2007random" role="doc-biblioref">Rahimi and Recht
2007</a>)</span>.</p>
<h2 id="precedents-and-the-novelty-gap">Precedents and the Novelty
Gap</h2>
<p>Random features have been used both to approximate attention kernels
in linear-time Transformers (e.g., Performer) <span class="citation"
data-cites="choromanski2021performer">(<a
href="#ref-choromanski2021performer" role="doc-biblioref">Choromanski et
al. 2021</a>)</span> and to build stochastic relative positional
encodings compatible with linear-complexity attention (e.g., Stochastic
Positional Encoding (SPE)) <span class="citation"
data-cites="liutkus2021relative">(<a href="#ref-liutkus2021relative"
role="doc-biblioref">Liutkus et al. 2021</a>)</span>. More recently,
structure-informed variants such as F-StrIPE extend these ideas for
domain-specific priors <span class="citation"
data-cites="agarwal2025fstripe">(<a href="#ref-agarwal2025fstripe"
role="doc-biblioref">Agarwal et al. 2025</a>)</span>. In parallel, work
on generalized RoPE mechanisms has explored learned or input-dependent
rotations (e.g., Selective RoPE) <span class="citation"
data-cites="movahedi2025selectiverope">(<a
href="#ref-movahedi2025selectiverope" role="doc-biblioref">Movahedi et
al. 2025</a>)</span>.</p>
<p>Our contribution is the synthesis: we identify the RoPE rotation
mechanism itself as a direct implementation of an RFF-style expectation,
provided the frequency distribution is centrally symmetric. This allows
the RFF kernel-design toolkit to be integrated into the efficient RoPE
operator as a general design methodology.</p>
<h1 id="the-rf-rope-framework">The RF-RoPE Framework</h1>
<p>We generalize the scalar RoPE rotation to arbitrary <span
class="math inline">\(k\)</span>-dimensional position vectors <span
class="math inline">\(\mathbf{p} \in \mathbb{R}^k\)</span>.</p>
<h2 id="generalized-rotational-inner-product">Generalized Rotational
Inner Product</h2>
<p>We define query <span class="math inline">\(\mathbf{q}_m \in
\mathbb{R}^d\)</span> and key <span class="math inline">\(\mathbf{k}_n
\in \mathbb{R}^d\)</span> vectors, associated with positions <span
class="math inline">\(\mathbf{p}_m\)</span> and <span
class="math inline">\(\mathbf{p}_n\)</span>. We split the feature
dimension <span class="math inline">\(d\)</span> into <span
class="math inline">\(D=d/2\)</span> pairs. For each pair <span
class="math inline">\(i \in \{1, \dots, D\}\)</span>, we draw a <span
class="math inline">\(k\)</span>-dimensional random frequency vector
<span class="math inline">\(\mathbf{\omega}_i \in \mathbb{R}^k\)</span>,
i.i.d. from a distribution <span
class="math inline">\(p(\mathbf{\omega})\)</span>.</p>
<p>The rotated query <span
class="math inline">\(\mathbf{q}&#39;_m\)</span> and key <span
class="math inline">\(\mathbf{k}&#39;_n\)</span> are formed by applying
a 2D rotation parameterized by the phase <span
class="math inline">\(\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{\omega}_i\)</span>: <span
class="math display">\[\mathbf{q}&#39;_{m, i} = R(\mathbf{p}_m \cdot
\mathbf{\omega}_i) \mathbf{q}_{m, i} \quad \text{and} \quad
\mathbf{k}&#39;_{n, i} = R(\mathbf{p}_n \cdot \mathbf{\omega}_i)
\mathbf{k}_{n, i}.\]</span> Letting <span class="math inline">\(\Delta
\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{p}_n - \mathbf{p}_m\)</span>, the local inner
product for pair <span class="math inline">\(i\)</span> simplifies to:
<span class="math display">\[S_i = (\mathbf{q}_{m, i}^T \mathbf{k}_{n,
i}) \cdot \cos(\Delta \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{\omega}_i) +
(\mathbf{q}_{m, i}^T \mathbf{\hat{k}}_{n, i}) \cdot \sin(\Delta
\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{\omega}_i),\]</span> where <span
class="math inline">\(\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{n, i}\)</span> is <span
class="math inline">\(\mathbf{k}_{n, i}\)</span> rotated by <span
class="math inline">\(+90^{\circ}\)</span>.</p>
<h2 id="the-master-theorem-for-rf-rope-kernels">The Master Theorem for
RF-RoPE Kernels</h2>
<p>We analyze the expected value of the attention score, <span
class="math inline">\(E_{\mathbf{\omega}}[S(\mathbf{p}_m,
\mathbf{p}_n)]\)</span>. Since <span
class="math inline">\(\sin(\cdot)\)</span> is an odd function, <span
class="math inline">\(E[\sin(\Delta \mathbf{p} \cdot
\mathbf{\omega})]=0\)</span> if the frequency distribution <span
class="math inline">\(p(\mathbf{\omega})\)</span> is centrally symmetric
(i.e., <span class="math inline">\(p(\mathbf{\omega}) =
p(-\mathbf{\omega})\)</span>).</p>
<p>This yields the Master Theorem: <span class="math display">\[\boxed{
E_{\mathbf{\omega}}[S(\mathbf{p}_m, \mathbf{p}_n)] = (\mathbf{q}_m^T
\mathbf{k}_n) \cdot \Phi(\Delta \mathbf{p})
}\]</span> where the resultant positional kernel <span
class="math inline">\(\Phi(\Delta \mathbf{p})\)</span> is the <span
class="math inline">\(k\)</span>-dimensional cosine transform of <span
class="math inline">\(p(\mathbf{\omega})\)</span>: <span
class="math display">\[\Phi(\Delta \mathbf{p}) = E[\cos(\Delta
\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{\omega})] = \int_{\mathbb{R}^k} \cos(\Delta
\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{\omega}) p(\mathbf{\omega})
d\mathbf{\omega}.\]</span> By Bochner’s theorem, this establishes the
inverse-design recipe: choose a desired symmetric, shift-invariant
kernel <span class="math inline">\(\Phi(\Delta \mathbf{p})\)</span> and
compute its (inverse) Fourier transform to obtain the required <span
class="math inline">\(p(\mathbf{\omega})\)</span> <span class="citation"
data-cites="bochner1955harmonic rasmussen2006gpml">(<a
href="#ref-bochner1955harmonic" role="doc-biblioref">Bochner 1955</a>;
<a href="#ref-rasmussen2006gpml" role="doc-biblioref">Rasmussen and
Williams 2006</a>)</span>, from which the frequencies <span
class="math inline">\(\mathbf{\omega}_i\)</span> must be sampled
(typically once at initialization).</p>
<h2 id="variance-analysis-and-convergence">Variance Analysis and
Convergence</h2>
<p>The Master Theorem holds in expectation. In practice, we use a finite
set of <span class="math inline">\(D=d/2\)</span> samples. We analyze
the variance of the attention score <span
class="math inline">\(S\)</span> as an estimator of <span
class="math inline">\(E[S]\)</span>.</p>
<p>The variance <span class="math inline">\(Var(S_i)\)</span> depends on
the content vectors and the kernel properties. Crucially, the variance
introduced by the sine terms is unavoidable in a single realization,
even though their expectation is zero.</p>
<p>The total variance of the score <span class="math inline">\(S = \sum
S_i\)</span> is <span class="math inline">\(\sum Var[S_i]\)</span>.
Since <span class="math inline">\(S\)</span> is the sum of <span
class="math inline">\(D\)</span> i.i.d. random variables with finite
variance, the Central Limit Theorem applies. The realized attention
score converges to the expected kernelized attention, with the
approximation error typically bounded by <span
class="math inline">\(O(1/\sqrt{D}) = O(1/\sqrt{d})\)</span>.</p>
<h1 id="application-to-rf-rope-design">Application to RF-RoPE
Design</h1>
<p>The RF-RoPE methodology allows us to realize various kernels by
sampling from their corresponding spectral densities <span
class="citation" data-cites="rahimi2007random rasmussen2006gpml">(<a
href="#ref-rahimi2007random" role="doc-biblioref">Rahimi and Recht
2007</a>; <a href="#ref-rasmussen2006gpml"
role="doc-biblioref">Rasmussen and Williams 2006</a>)</span>.</p>
<h4 id="gaussian-rbf-kernel">Gaussian (RBF) Kernel:</h4>
<p><span class="math inline">\(\Phi(\Delta \mathbf{p}) =
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \| \Delta \mathbf{p}
\|^2\right)\)</span>. Requires sampling <span
class="math inline">\(\mathbf{\omega}\)</span> from a Gaussian
distribution <span class="math inline">\(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},
\sigma^{-2} I_k)\)</span>. Enforces strong, smooth locality <span
class="citation" data-cites="rasmussen2006gpml">(<a
href="#ref-rasmussen2006gpml" role="doc-biblioref">Rasmussen and
Williams 2006</a>)</span>.</p>
<h4 id="cauchy-kernel-1d">Cauchy Kernel (1D):</h4>
<p><span class="math inline">\(\Phi(\Delta m) = (1 + (\Delta m /
b)^2)^{-1}\)</span>. Requires sampling <span
class="math inline">\(\omega\)</span> from a Laplace distribution <span
class="math inline">\(p(\omega) \propto \exp(-|\omega|/b)\)</span> <span
class="citation" data-cites="rasmussen2006gpml">(<a
href="#ref-rasmussen2006gpml" role="doc-biblioref">Rasmussen and
Williams 2006</a>)</span>. Allows for heavy-tailed (global)
interactions.</p>
<h4 id="sinc-kernel">Sinc Kernel:</h4>
<p><span class="math inline">\(\Phi(\Delta \mathbf{p}) = \prod
\text{sinc}(W_j \cdot \Delta p_j)\)</span>. Requires sampling <span
class="math inline">\(\mathbf{\omega}\)</span> from a Uniform
distribution <span class="math inline">\(U(-W_j, W_j)\)</span>.
Corresponds to an ideal band-limited filter.</p>
<h4 id="matérn-kernel">Matérn Kernel:</h4>
<p>A flexible family parameterized by <span
class="math inline">\(\nu\)</span>, interpolating between Exponential
and Gaussian kernels <span class="citation"
data-cites="matern1960spatial rasmussen2006gpml">(<a
href="#ref-matern1960spatial" role="doc-biblioref">Matérn 1960</a>; <a
href="#ref-rasmussen2006gpml" role="doc-biblioref">Rasmussen and
Williams 2006</a>)</span>. Its spectral density corresponds to
(multivariate) Student-<span class="math inline">\(t\)</span>-like
tails, motivating sampling schemes based on heavy-tailed frequency
distributions.</p>
<h1 id="analytical-re-assessment-of-standard-rope">Analytical
Re-Assessment of Standard RoPE</h1>
<p>The standard, deterministic RoPE (for <span
class="math inline">\(k=1\)</span>) uses a fixed geometric progression
of frequencies <span class="citation" data-cites="su2021roformer">(<a
href="#ref-su2021roformer" role="doc-biblioref">Su et al.
2021</a>)</span>: <span class="math inline">\(\theta_i =
B^{-2i/d}\)</span>.</p>
<h2 id="asymmetry-and-the-complex-kernel">Asymmetry and the Complex
Kernel</h2>
<p>In standard RoPE, the frequencies <span
class="math inline">\(\theta_i\)</span> are fixed and strictly positive
<span class="citation" data-cites="su2021roformer">(<a
href="#ref-su2021roformer" role="doc-biblioref">Su et al.
2021</a>)</span>. The attention score is: <span
class="math display">\[S(m,n) = \sum_{i=1}^{D} \left[ C_i \cos(\Delta m
\cdot \theta_i) + X_i \sin(\Delta m \cdot \theta_i) \right].\]</span>
Since the set of frequencies is not symmetric around zero, the sine term
does not vanish. The resulting kernel is complex and lacks the simple
multiplicative form. This highlights the elegance of the RF-RoPE
approach, which guarantees a real-valued, multiplicative kernel in
expectation by design.</p>
<h2 id="the-implied-frequency-distribution-and-slow-decay">The Implied
Frequency Distribution and Slow Decay</h2>
<p>We analyze the decay properties of standard RoPE by determining the
continuous distribution <span class="math inline">\(p(\theta)\)</span>
that the discrete set <span class="math inline">\(\{\theta_i\}\)</span>
approximates. The density of frequencies is proportional to the
derivative of the index <span class="math inline">\(i\)</span> with
respect to the frequency <span class="math inline">\(\theta\)</span>:
<span class="math display">\[p(\theta) \propto \left| \frac{di}{d\theta}
\right| \propto \frac{1}{\theta}.\]</span> The implied frequency
distribution of standard RoPE follows a <span
class="math inline">\(p(\theta) \propto 1/\theta\)</span> distribution.
This is a heavy-tailed distribution that heavily emphasizes lower
frequencies.</p>
<p>The resulting kernel <span class="math inline">\(\Phi(\Delta
m)\)</span> (the cosine transform) exhibits very slow decay. This helps
explain why standard RoPE can struggle to enforce strong locality bias
without additional modifications <span class="citation"
data-cites="su2021roformer">(<a href="#ref-su2021roformer"
role="doc-biblioref">Su et al. 2021</a>)</span>.</p>
<h1
id="beyond-initialization-learned-adaptations-and-shifted-attention">Beyond
Initialization: Learned Adaptations and Shifted Attention</h1>
<p>The RF-RoPE framework provides a methodology for designing the
positional kernel at initialization. However, Transformers often develop
specialized heads that attend at fixed, non-zero offsets (e.g.,
induction heads) <span class="citation"
data-cites="olsson2022incontext">(<a href="#ref-olsson2022incontext"
role="doc-biblioref">Olsson et al. 2022</a>)</span>. This section
analyzes how the architecture learns such “shifted-local” attention.</p>
<h2 id="the-locality-constraint-of-the-master-theorem">The Locality
Constraint of the Master Theorem</h2>
<p>The kernels designed via the Master Theorem (Section 3.2) are
inherently centered at the origin.</p>
<div class="theorem">
<p><strong>Theorem 1</strong> (Peak at Origin). <em>A kernel <span
class="math inline">\(\Phi(\Delta \mathbf{p})\)</span> derived from the
cosine transform of a probability distribution <span
class="math inline">\(p(\mathbf{\omega})\)</span> achieves its global
maximum at <span class="math inline">\(\Delta \mathbf{p} =
\mathbf{0}\)</span>.</em></p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> <span class="math inline">\(\Phi(\mathbf{0}) =
E[\cos(0)] = 1\)</span>. For any <span class="math inline">\(\Delta
\mathbf{p} \neq \mathbf{0}\)</span>, since <span
class="math inline">\(\cos(x) \le 1\)</span>, the expectation <span
class="math inline">\(E[\cos(\Delta \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{\omega})]
\le 1\)</span>. Therefore, <span class="math inline">\(\Phi(\Delta
\mathbf{p}) \le \Phi(\mathbf{0})\)</span>. ◻</p>
</div>
<p>This proves that we cannot design a shifted-local kernel merely by
selecting a different frequency distribution <span
class="math inline">\(p(\mathbf{\omega})\)</span>. The adaptation must
occur within the learned parameters.</p>
<h2 id="the-mechanism-of-learned-phase-shifts">The Mechanism of Learned
Phase Shifts</h2>
<p>The capacity for shifted attention arises from the adaptability of
the query and key projection matrices (<span
class="math inline">\(W_Q\)</span> and <span
class="math inline">\(W_K\)</span>). These matrices are applied before
the RoPE rotation.</p>
<p>We hypothesize that during training, the projection matrices <span
class="math inline">\(W_{Q,i}\)</span> and <span
class="math inline">\(W_{K,i}\)</span> learn to implement a fixed
rotation within the 2D subspace corresponding to frequency <span
class="math inline">\(\mathbf{\omega}_i\)</span>. Let these learned
rotations be <span class="math inline">\(R(\phi_{q,i})\)</span> and
<span class="math inline">\(R(\phi_{k,i})\)</span>.</p>
<p>The effective transformation becomes (where <span
class="math inline">\(\mathbf{v}_{m,i}, \mathbf{v}_{n,i}\)</span> are
base content vectors): <span class="math display">\[\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{q}&#39;_{m,i} &amp;\approx R(\mathbf{p}_m \cdot
\mathbf{\omega}_i) [ R(\phi_{q,i}) \mathbf{v}_{m,i} ] \\
\mathbf{k}&#39;_{n,i} &amp;\approx R(\mathbf{p}_n \cdot
\mathbf{\omega}_i) [ R(\phi_{k,i}) \mathbf{v}_{n,i} ].
\end{aligned}\]</span> Since 2D rotations commute: <span
class="math display">\[\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{q}&#39;_{m,i} &amp;= R(\mathbf{p}_m \cdot \mathbf{\omega}_i +
\phi_{q,i}) \mathbf{v}_{m,i} \\
\mathbf{k}&#39;_{n,i} &amp;= R(\mathbf{p}_n \cdot \mathbf{\omega}_i +
\phi_{k,i}) \mathbf{v}_{n,i}.
\end{aligned}\]</span> The attention score <span
class="math inline">\(S_i\)</span> depends on the relative angle: <span
class="math display">\[\Delta\text{Angle}_i = (\Delta \mathbf{p} \cdot
\mathbf{\omega}_i) + (\phi_{k,i} - \phi_{q,i}).\]</span> Let the net
Learned Phase Shift be <span class="math inline">\(\Phi_i = \phi_{k,i} -
\phi_{q,i}\)</span>. The attention score for this pair is: <span
class="math display">\[S_i(\Delta \mathbf{p}) = (\mathbf{v}_{m,i}^T
\mathbf{v}_{n,i}) \cdot \cos(\Delta \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{\omega}_i +
\Phi_i).\]</span></p>
<h2 id="derivation-of-the-shifted-local-kernel">Derivation of the
Shifted-Local Kernel</h2>
<p>The total attention score for this head is: <span
class="math display">\[S(\Delta \mathbf{p}) = \sum_{i=1}^{D} C_i
\cos(\Delta \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{\omega}_i + \Phi_i).\]</span> If
the training objective incentivizes this head to focus attention at a
specific relative position <span class="math inline">\(\Delta \mathbf{p}
= -\mathbf{L}\)</span>, the optimizer will adjust the weights to
maximize <span class="math inline">\(S(-\mathbf{L})\)</span>. This
requires the argument of the cosine to be zero: <span
class="math display">\[-\mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{\omega}_i + \Phi_i
\approx 0 \implies \Phi_i \approx \mathbf{L} \cdot
\mathbf{\omega}_i.\]</span> The network learns a phase shift
proportional to the dot product of the frequency vector and the desired
shift vector. Substituting this back yields the form of the learned
shifted-local kernel: <span class="math display">\[\boxed{
S(\Delta \mathbf{p}) \approx \sum_{i=1}^{D} C_i \cos( (\Delta \mathbf{p}
+ \mathbf{L}) \cdot \mathbf{\omega}_i )
}.\]</span> This demonstrates that while the RF-RoPE framework dictates
the kernel at initialization (where <span
class="math inline">\(\Phi_i=0\)</span>), the RoPE mechanism is flexible
enough to allow the learned weight matrices to recenter the kernel at
any desired location <span class="math inline">\(\Delta \mathbf{p} =
-\mathbf{L}\)</span>. The shape of this shifted kernel will depend on
the newly learned coefficients <span class="math inline">\(C_i\)</span>,
which may differ from the local kernel, potentially allowing for sharper
concentration if required by the task.</p>
<h1 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h1>
<p>The Random Frequency RoPE (RF-RoPE) framework transcends RoPE’s fixed
design limitations by establishing a principled, generative design
methodology rooted in Bochner’s theorem <span class="citation"
data-cites="bochner1955harmonic rasmussen2006gpml">(<a
href="#ref-bochner1955harmonic" role="doc-biblioref">Bochner 1955</a>;
<a href="#ref-rasmussen2006gpml" role="doc-biblioref">Rasmussen and
Williams 2006</a>)</span>. By framing the frequency list as a sample
from a designed spectral density <span
class="math inline">\(p(\mathbf{\omega})\)</span>, we enable the
implementation of any desired shift-invariant kernel <span
class="math inline">\(\Phi(\Delta \mathbf{p})\)</span> as an expected
attention modulation at initialization, using the RFF design toolkit
<span class="citation" data-cites="rahimi2007random">(<a
href="#ref-rahimi2007random" role="doc-biblioref">Rahimi and Recht
2007</a>)</span>. This shifts the paradigm of positional encoding design
from empirical tuning to mathematically precise kernel engineering.
Furthermore, we have demonstrated that the underlying rotational
mechanism is flexible enough to allow the model to learn adaptations
during training, such as shifted-local attention via learned phase
shifts in the <span class="math inline">\(W_Q\)</span> and <span
class="math inline">\(W_K\)</span> matrices, providing a comprehensive
understanding of the RoPE mechanism both at initialization and after
training.</p>
